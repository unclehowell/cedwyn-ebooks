The method

“Here is a verbal contraption. How does it work?” The book asks that question of the official record, not whether a judge, a registrar or a clerk was right. Each passage is set out in its own words, dated, with its catalogue number. Where two offices, or one office at two dates, word the same matter differently, the versions are laid side by side and not reconciled.

Documented

Words quoted from a record, with its date and its evidence-catalogue number.

Family’s position

The family’s reading of those words, always labelled, never presented as a finding.

Not checked

Anything the record held for this edition does not settle, named in each chapter’s “where this may be wrong”.

Six patterns, in the record’s own words

  1. Notice. Two days between the landlords’ notice (17 Sep 1974) and leave to enforce (19 Sep 1974), while the occupier’s pleaded defence was left part-heard: “That action has never been finally heard.”
  2. Names. The occupier “referred to”, “sued as” and “described again as” a second name; in 2026 the Registry called the point one “heard by the Court”.
  3. “Conceived the notion”. The court’s phrase for the family’s title case, beside its own “expressed belief”.
  4. A licence nobody accepted. “Unilaterally”, “never expressly accepted”, “artificial”, “a new one”, “whether she liked it or not”, later retold as “permission”.
  5. The Registry’s six replies. From “look behind” and “would have dealt with” to “registered correctly”, “correct any error” and “the appropriate forum … is the Court”.
  6. The name. “Ty-mawr” and “Great house” in the nineteenth-century public record; the “Farm” suffix from estate papers of 1806; Tŷ Mawr 0 times and “Welsh” 0 times in the 1987 judgment.

What format is it?

What is the method?

Close reading. Each chapter takes a short passage of the record and asks how its wording works: which word carries the weight, who is made the actor, what the sentence takes for granted and what it leaves out. Every sentence of comment stands on the wording itself, a dated record with its catalogue number, a count, or the family’s position, labelled as such. Where a record has not been seen, the book writes “not checked”.

Does it take sides?

It sets out the family’s lines of argument (procedural unfairness, the court’s wording, a miscarriage of justice, the Land Registry’s wording, and the treatment of a Welsh house) and labels each reading as the family’s position. Each chapter then sets out, just as plainly, what cuts against it.
